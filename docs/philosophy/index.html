The biggest questions people have ever asked, in plain English. For every philosopher and school: the idea, an everyday example, how to use it today, and where it's been criticized.

Philosophers

  • SocratesAthens, c. 470–399 BCEThe man who walked around the marketplace asking people questions until they realized they didn't know what they thought they knew.
  • PlatoAthens, c. 427–347 BCEHe believed what we see with our eyes is only shadows, and that the real thing lives in a world of unchanging Forms that we reach through reason.
  • AristotleGreece, 384–322 BCE“The First Teacher”: the first to lay out the rules of logic, and the thinker who saw virtue as a middle point between two extremes.
  • EpicurusGreece, 341–270 BCEHe said the goal of life is happiness, but for him happiness meant peace of mind, not partying.
  • Avicenna (Ibn Sina)Bukhara and Hamadan, 980–1037 CEThe physician-philosopher whose medical textbook was taught in Europe for centuries, and who asked: if you were created floating in midair with no sensations at all, would you still know you exist?
  • Al-GhazaliTus and Baghdad, 1058–1111 CEThe scholar who doubted everything he knew in order to find certainty, and wrote about his crisis of doubt with rare honesty.
  • Averroes (Ibn Rushd)Córdoba and Marrakesh, 1126–1198 CEThe judge and philosopher who explained Aristotle to the world, and argued that careful reasoning and religious belief don't have to be enemies.
  • Ibn KhaldunTunis and Cairo, 1332–1406 CEOne of the first people to ask why states rise and fall, and to insist that a historian should question a report before passing it on.
  • René DescartesFrance, the Netherlands and Sweden, 1596–1650He decided to doubt everything he possibly could, and found one thing he couldn't doubt: that he was thinking.
  • David HumeScotland, 1711–1776He pointed out that the sun rising every day so far isn't logical proof it will rise tomorrow, and that we live by habit more than by reason.
  • Immanuel KantGermany (Prussia), 1724–1804He said the right thing to do is whatever you could accept as a rule for everyone, and that a person is always an end in themselves, never just a means.
  • Friedrich NietzscheGermany, 1844–1900The philosopher who challenged people to re-examine every value they inherited, and to live a life they'd be glad to repeat forever.
  • Al-FarabiFarab, Baghdad and Damascus, c. 870–950 CECalled “the Second Teacher” after Aristotle, he believed a good city is one whose purpose is the happiness of the people who live in it.
  • Ibn TufaylAl-Andalus and Marrakesh, early 12th century–1185 CEHe wrote the story of a child growing up alone on an island who reaches life's biggest questions through his own reasoning and observation.
  • Taha HusseinMinya, Cairo and Paris, 1889–1973Known as “the Dean of Arabic Literature,” he applied methodical doubt to inherited tradition and fought to make education a right for everyone.
  • Zaki Naguib MahmoudDamietta, Cairo and London, 1905–1993The Egyptian philosopher who argued that any sentence you can't check by experience has no meaning at all, and later went back to his own heritage looking for what was still useful.
  • Abdel Rahman BadawiDamietta, Cairo and Paris, 1917–2002The Egyptian philosopher who brought existentialism into Arabic, and believed every person is a unique existence, not a number in a crowd.
  • John LockeEngland, 1632–1704He said the mind is born like a blank sheet of paper, and that a government's legitimacy comes from the consent of the people.
  • John Stuart MillLondon and Avignon, 1806–1873He said your freedom ends only where it harms someone else, and that the majority can be as much of a tyrant as any king.
  • Ludwig WittgensteinVienna and Cambridge, 1889–1951He argued that many philosophical problems come from misunderstanding language, and that the meaning of a word is how it's used.
  • Hypatia of AlexandriaAlexandria, born c. 355–370, died 415 CEThe mathematician and astronomer who taught philosophy in Alexandria, drawing students from far-off places.
  • Simone de BeauvoirParis, 1908–1986She said one is not born a woman but becomes one: society is what writes much of the role.
  • Hannah ArendtGermany and New York, 1906–1975She argued that great evil can be done by very ordinary people, not monsters, who simply stopped thinking.

Schools and ideas

  • StoicismGreece and Rome, from the 3rd century BCESeparate what's in your control from what isn't, and spend your energy only on the first.
  • SkepticismFrom Pyrrho in ancient Greece to todayHold off on judging until the evidence is strong enough, and don't rush to say “definitely.”
  • RationalismEurope, 17th centuryReason is the main source of knowledge, and some things can be known without testing them.
  • EmpiricismBritain, 17th and 18th centuriesEverything we know comes from experience and the senses; the mind starts as a blank slate.
  • UtilitarianismBritain, late 18th and 19th centuriesThe right action is the one that brings the greatest good to the greatest number of people.
  • Virtue EthicsFrom Aristotle to todayDon't only ask “What should I do?” Ask “What kind of person do I want to be?”
  • Social ContractEurope, 17th and 18th centuriesGovernments and laws exist because people agreed, at least implicitly, to give up a little freedom in exchange for safety.
  • ExistentialismEurope, 19th and 20th centuriesNobody is going to hand you a ready-made meaning for your life. You're free, and you're responsible for what you do with that freedom.
  • AbsurdismFrance, 20th centuryWe search for meaning, and the universe stays silent. The answer isn't despair; it's to live fully awake anyway.
  • PragmatismUnited States, late 19th centuryThe meaning of an idea is the difference it makes in practice. If it makes no difference, arguing about it is a waste of time.
  • Moral RelativismFrom the Greek Sophists to todayDo right and wrong change from one society to another, or are some things wrong everywhere and always?
  • Falsifiability (Karl Popper)Karl Popper, 20th centuryA scientific theory is one that could be proven wrong. A theory that explains everything explains nothing.
  • CynicismGreece, from the 4th century BCELive simply and without pretense, and ask of every custom: does this actually serve me, or am I just going along with it?
  • DeontologyFrom Kant in the 18th century to todaySome things are wrong even when the outcome is good, and some things are your duty even when they're not in your interest.
  • Logical Positivism (The Vienna Circle)Vienna, 1920s and 1930sA sentence has meaning only if you can check it by experience or it's true by definition. Everything else is empty talk.
  • PhenomenologyGermany and France, from the early 20th centuryBefore you ask “What is this thing really?”, describe carefully how it shows up for you.
  • Determinism and Free WillAn old debate, from the Stoics to today's neuroscienceIf everything has a cause before it, where is there room for your choice? And can we still hold anyone responsible?
  • ConfucianismChina, from the 6th and 5th centuries BCEA good society starts with good people, and good people are built through ethics and courtesy in everyday dealings.
  • Ethics of CareUnited States, from the 1980sMorality isn't only rules and calculations. It's also seeing the person in front of you and paying attention to them.
  • Renaissance HumanismItaly, then Europe, 14th to 16th centuriesGo back to the original sources, and learn to write, speak and think well, because education is what shapes a person.
