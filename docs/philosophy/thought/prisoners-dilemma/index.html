The Prisoner's Dilemma

Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation, 1950; Albert Tucker framed it as a story about two prisoners and gave it its name

You and your buddy get arrested, and the police put each of you in a separate room. The detective tells you: if you testify against your buddy and he stays quiet, you walk free and he gets 10 years. If you both testify against each other, you each get 5. If you both stay quiet, you each get just 1 year. Your buddy is hearing the same offer, and you have no idea what he'll do.

Do you stay quiet or testify against your buddy?

What did philosophers say?

Game theory
By cold math, testifying is better for you no matter what your buddy does: if he stays quiet, you walk; if he testifies, you get 5 instead of 10. The problem is that he's doing the same math, so you both end up with 5, when staying quiet together would have gotten you just 1 each.
Thomas Hobbes (social contract)
Many scholars see this as close to Hobbes's idea of the “state of nature”: without rules everyone respects and an authority to back them up, everyone looks out for himself and everyone loses. That's why people agree to laws that make trust possible.
Axelrod's tournaments (around 1980)
Robert Axelrod ran a contest between computer programs that played this game many times in a row. The winner was a simple strategy submitted by Anatol Rapoport called “Tit for Tat”: start by cooperating, then do whatever the other player did last round.

Why it matters today

When every owner in the condo waits for someone else to chip in first to fix the elevator, or when every driver pulls into the intersection because “if I don't, someone else will” and the whole thing gridlocks, that's a prisoner's dilemma. The lesson: when the situation repeats and people know each other, cooperating is the truly smart move.
