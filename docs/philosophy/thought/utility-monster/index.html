The Utility Monster

Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974

Imagine a strange creature living in your neighborhood who feels happiness many times more intensely than any human. If you eat a slice of cake, you're a little happy. If it eats the slice, it's a thousand times happier than you. Same with money, time and houses. No matter how much we take from ourselves and give to it, the happiness it gains outweighs what we lose.

If our goal is the most happiness in the world, do we give the monster everything?

What did philosophers say?

Robert Nozick
He invented the monster to expose a problem with utilitarianism: if all that matters is total happiness, we'd have to sacrifice everyone for one creature that feels more. That looks wrong, so something must be missing from the theory.
John Rawls
Three years earlier, in A Theory of Justice, he'd made a related point: utilitarianism treats society as if it were one big person, and doesn't take seriously that each of us is a separate individual with a life of our own. The monster's joy doesn't make up for someone else's misery.
Defenders of utilitarianism
They reply that the monster is impossible in real life, because the more anyone has, the less each extra bit makes them happy. And our gut reactions to sci-fi stories like this aren't strong evidence. A related view that Derek Parfit laid out and was sympathetic to, called “prioritarianism,” gives extra weight to the happiness of those who are worse off, which shrinks the monster's advantage a lot, though not always completely.

Why it matters today

In some families, one sibling is loud and slow to calm down, so everyone keeps the peace at everyone else's expense. At work, one “more important” department eats the whole budget. And as people start asking whether AI might someday “feel,” the question comes back: should whoever feels the most get the most? Nozick's answer: each person has rights, not just a share of the total.
