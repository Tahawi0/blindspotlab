The Brain in a Vat

A modern version of Descartes' demon, made famous by Hilary Putnam in Reason, Truth and History, 1981

Imagine a scientist removes your brain while you sleep and puts it in a vat of fluid that keeps it alive. He wires its nerves to a powerful computer that sends exactly the signals your eyes, ears and hands used to send. Right now you “feel” awake, heading to work, stuck in traffic. None of it is real. And from the inside, there's no way to tell the difference.

Can you prove you're not a brain in a vat?

What did philosophers say?

The skeptics
They say: since everything you experience would be identical either way, you have no evidence that tells the two apart. And if you can't rule out being a brain in a vat, then you don't really “know” you have hands.
Hilary Putnam
He offered a clever reply. For a brain in a vat, the word “vat” refers to the computer's images, not to real vats, because it has never dealt with a real one. So if it says “I am a brain in a vat,” what it says comes out false. The sentence undermines itself.
G. E. Moore
He answered with common sense. He held up his hand and said, “Here is one hand.” He thought we're more certain of something like that than of any philosophical argument claiming otherwise. So if an argument concludes you have no hands, the problem is with the argument.

Why it matters today

With VR and computer-generated video, the gap between “real” and “fake” shrinks every day. This thought experiment teaches you to ask: what evidence actually tells the two apart? And if there isn't any, keep your confidence in line with the evidence.
