Buridan's Donkey

Attributed to the French philosopher Jean Buridan (14th century), though the donkey doesn't appear in his writings, and the idea goes back to Aristotle

A very hungry donkey stands exactly halfway between two piles of hay. The piles are the same size, smell the same and sit the same distance away. There's no reason to choose one over the other. So he stands there looking left, then right, left, then right. And he starves to death with a week's worth of food on either side of him.

When two options are exactly equal, what do you do?

What did philosophers say?

Al-Ghazali
In The Incoherence of the Philosophers, this 11th-century thinker gave the example of a hungry man facing two dates that are exactly alike. The man will obviously take one, he said, because the will itself can choose between two equal things without any outside reason to tip it.
Leibniz
He held that nothing happens without a sufficient reason. Perfect equality like this never actually happens, he said, because the world isn't split into two identical halves. There's always some small difference nudging you one way, even if you don't notice it.
By pragmatist logic
If the two options make no practical difference, arguing with yourself is pointless, and the only real loss is the time you waste. Pick one, or flip a coin, and eat.

Why it matters today

Standing in a store for 20 minutes between two nearly identical shirts. Scrolling a streaming app for 40 minutes and never picking a show. We often lose more to indecision than we would by choosing “wrong.” If the difference is small, give yourself a deadline and stick to it.
