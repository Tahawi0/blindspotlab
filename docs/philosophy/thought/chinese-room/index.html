Thought Experiments

The Chinese Room

John Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs,” 1980

You're locked in a room, and you don't know a single character of Chinese. You have a huge rulebook in English: “If you get this symbol, answer with that symbol.” Through a slot in the door, people pass you notes written in Chinese. You look things up in the book and pass back the reply. The people outside read the answers and say, “Whoever's in there clearly speaks Chinese.” But inside, you don't understand a word.

If a computer answers exactly like a person, does it understand?

What did philosophers say?

John Searle
He said a computer does exactly what you did in the room: it moves symbols around according to rules. Moving symbols, by itself, doesn't produce meaning. So a program can imitate understanding without understanding anything.
The “systems reply”
Many people answered Searle: sure, you alone don't understand, but the whole room does, meaning you, the book and the notes together. Searle replied in the same paper: fine, suppose I memorize the whole book and become the entire system. I still won't understand Chinese.
Alan Turing
Thirty years earlier, in 1950, he proposed setting aside the vague question “Can machines think?” and asking instead: if you can't tell a machine's answers from a person's in a written conversation, what grounds do you have for saying it isn't thinking?

Why it matters today

Today there are AI programs that write your emails, explain lessons and even comfort you. So the question is practical now: when a program says “I understand how you feel,” does it really? Your answer shapes how much you trust it and lean on it.
