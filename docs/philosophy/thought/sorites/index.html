The Paradox of the Heap

Attributed to Eubulides of Miletus, of the Megarian school in Greece, 4th century BCE

In front of you is a big heap of rice. Take away one grain. Still a heap? Of course. Another grain? Still a heap. No single grain makes a difference. Now keep going, grain by grain. Eventually you're left with one grain, and your logic says it's still a “heap,” because no single step changed anything. Where's the mistake?

Is there one particular grain where the heap stops being a heap?

What did philosophers say?

Chrysippus the Stoic
According to the reports we have, his advice was this: if someone keeps asking “Still a heap?” grain by grain, stop answering a little before you reach the zone where you're unsure. Better to stay silent than to say something false.
Timothy Williamson
In his 1994 book Vagueness, he defended a surprising view: there really is exactly one grain that separates “heap” from “not a heap,” but we can never know which one. The vagueness is in our knowledge, not in the word.
“Degrees of truth” theorists
They argue that “This is a heap” doesn't have to be 100% true or 100% false. It can be 90% true, then 60%, then 10%. Close to this idea is the “fuzzy logic” that Lotfi Zadeh developed starting in the 1960s.

Why it matters today

“One cigarette won't matter.” “One more hour of overtime is fine.” “One little cookie won't wreck the diet.” Each step really is small, but small steps make a heap. When you catch yourself saying “just this once,” ask: where am I drawing the line, or is there no line at all?
