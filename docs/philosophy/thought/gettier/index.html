Gettier Cases

Edmund Gettier, “Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?,” 1963

You and your coworker Jones are up for the same promotion. Your boss tells you herself that Jones is getting it, and you just watched Jones count ten coins in his pocket. So you conclude: “The person who gets the promotion has ten coins in his pocket.” The next day, surprise: the boss changed her mind, and you got the promotion. And when you reach into your own pocket, you find ten coins you didn't know were there.

Did you “know” that the person getting the promotion had ten coins in his pocket?

What did philosophers say?

Edmund Gettier
The classic definition of knowledge was a belief that is true and justified. In a paper only three pages long, he described two cases like this one: all three conditions are met, yet nobody would say you “knew.” So the definition is incomplete.
Michael Clark (1963)
That same year, he suggested the problem is that your conclusion rests on a false belief: that Jones would get the promotion. So add a condition: knowledge can't depend on any false belief. But later philosophers came up with cases that involve no false belief at all, and there's still no knowledge.
Timothy Williamson
He thinks philosophers spent decades hunting for the “fourth condition” and never found it because knowledge can't be broken down into simpler parts. Knowledge itself is the basic thing, and belief is what we understand through it.

Why it matters today

The sports-radio caller who says “Told you so!” because his upset pick came true, even though his reasons were totally wrong. Gettier teaches you to tell the difference between someone who “turned out right” and someone who “actually knew.” The first can be luck. Only the second deserves your trust next time.
