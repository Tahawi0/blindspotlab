School of Thought

Cynicism

Greece, from the 4th century BCE

Live simply and without pretense, and ask of every custom: does this actually serve me, or am I just going along with it?

The idea

It started with Antisthenes, a student of Socrates, and its most famous figure is Diogenes of Sinope. The name comes from the Greek word for “dog,” because the Cynics lived in the street and didn't care what anyone said about them. The idea: happiness means living “according to nature” and doing without money and appearances. Stories say Diogenes slept in a large storage jar and threw away his cup when he saw a child drinking from cupped hands. Those stories were written down centuries after him, so not all of them are reliable.

In real life

Your brother's wedding is coming up, and everyone says you “have to” have a big venue and 300 guests, even if it means putting it on credit cards. A Cynic would ask: “Have to? Says who? And what do the two people getting married actually want?”

Use it today

Pick a habit you keep because “everyone does it,” and ask: if nobody would ever see me, would I still do this? If not, think about dropping it.

The criticism

Their shock-value style can slide into plain rudeness with no point. Giving up on society entirely is easy for a single man and hard for a parent with a household to support. And today “cynic” means someone who trusts no one, which is far from the original.
