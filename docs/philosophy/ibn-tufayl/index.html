Philosopher

Ibn Tufayl

Al-Andalus and Marrakesh, early 12th century–1185 CE

He wrote the story of a child growing up alone on an island who reaches life's biggest questions through his own reasoning and observation.

The idea

Ibn Tufayl was a physician and court official for the Almohad rulers in Marrakesh, and he's the one who introduced Averroes to the caliph. His best-known work is a philosophical novel, Hayy ibn Yaqzan (roughly “Alive, Son of Awake”): a boy raised by a gazelle on an island with no other people. When the gazelle dies, he opens her body to find out what had kept her alive. Through observation and trial and error he learns about fire, animals and the stars, and eventually reasons his way to questions about the origin of the universe. The story asks: can a human reach knowledge through reason alone? It was translated into Latin in 1671 and spread across Europe.

In real life

A kid takes the TV remote apart, piece by piece, to see how it works. His parents yell at him, but he's doing exactly what Hayy did: asking a question and going after the answer with his own hands.

Use it today

Pick something you use every day without understanding how it works, like the fridge or your Wi-Fi. First try to figure it out by observation, then look it up and compare your guess with the real answer.

The criticism

What we know today about children raised in isolation suggests they don't develop language or complex thinking on their own. The mind needs other people. The end of the story also implies that most people can't handle the truth, which is a pretty elitist view.
