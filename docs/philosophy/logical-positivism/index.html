School of Thought

Logical Positivism (The Vienna Circle)

Vienna, 1920s and 1930s

A sentence has meaning only if you can check it by experience or it's true by definition. Everything else is empty talk.

The idea

A group of scientists and philosophers known as the Vienna Circle, including Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap and Otto Neurath, published their manifesto in 1929. Influenced by the early Wittgenstein, they wanted philosophy to be as precise as science. So they said a sentence has meaning if it can be checked by observation, or if it's true by definition, like math. Anything else, including a lot of metaphysics, simply has no meaning. A. J. Ayer brought the idea to English readers in 1936, and Zaki Naguib Mahmoud brought it into Arabic.

In real life

An argument at the sports bar: “This is the best pizza in Chicago.” A positivist would ask what “best” means. “Cheapest” and “sells the most pies” can be checked with numbers. “Tastes best to me” is your taste, not a fact.

Use it today

When a discussion starts heating up, ask: are we disagreeing about a fact we can check, or about taste? If it's a fact, go find the evidence. If it's taste, the argument will never end.

The criticism

The principle “a sentence must be checkable by experience” can't itself be checked by experience, so it trips over itself. Popper argued that what separates science from non-science is falsifiability, not verification, and he rejected the idea that non-scientific talk is meaningless. In 1951 W. V. O. Quine attacked the sharp line between the two kinds of statements. Over time the movement faded.
