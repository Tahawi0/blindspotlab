Skepticism

From Pyrrho in ancient Greece to today

Hold off on judging until the evidence is strong enough, and don't rush to say “definitely.”

The idea

Ancient skeptics like Pyrrho believed every argument has an equally strong argument against it, so the wise move is to suspend judgment and find peace in that. Later, thinkers like Al-Ghazali and Descartes used philosophical doubt as a tool for reaching certainty. Today, “scientific skepticism” simply means: don't accept a claim without evidence that matches the size of the claim.

In real life

Someone posts in the family group chat that “celery juice on an empty stomach cures high blood pressure for good.” A skeptic won't say it's a lie, and won't say it's true. A skeptic asks: where's the evidence, and who tested it? (And for anything health-related, the person to ask is a doctor, not the group chat.)

Use it today

Keep three boxes instead of two: “true,” “false,” and “don't know yet.” That third box will protect you from most rumors.

The criticism

Total skepticism undercuts itself: if nothing is certain, then “nothing is certain” isn't certain either. And too much doubt can turn into rejecting things even when the evidence for them is strong.
