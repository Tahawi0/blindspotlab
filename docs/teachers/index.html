For teachers and parents

Blind Spot Lab in the Classroom

6 ready-to-teach critical thinking lessons, 45 minutes each, built for grades 6–12. Every lesson has a learning goal, a warm-up, a Blind Spot Lab game students play on their phones or on the classroom screen, discussion questions, and a printable activity sheet.

How to use it

  • No accounts or sign-ups needed. Students just open the link and play.
  • No phones? Put the game on the classroom screen and have the whole class vote on each answer.
  • Print the activity sheet with the "Print lesson" button, and choose "Save as PDF" if you want a copy on your computer.
  • All examples are everyday and made up, with no real names. Preview "The Family Group Chat" game before class, since it includes situations about money and health.

The lessons are free. If your school would like a workshop or a version tailored to its curriculum, get in touch.

Lesson 1 · 45 minutes

What Is a Fallacy?

Goal: Students tell the difference between answering what someone said and attacking the person who said it, and learn to recognize the straw man and ad hominem.

1. Warm-up (5 minutes)

Ask the class: "Your friend says the cafeteria food is overpriced, and someone answers, 'Like you know anything about food.'" Does that reply answer the point or attack the person?

2. Play (20 minutes)

Fallacy of the Day Fallacy Court

3. Discuss (10 minutes)

  • When do we attack the person instead of their point? Why does that feel convincing?
  • How can you respond to someone who twisted your words without starting a fight?

4. Worksheet (10 minutes)

For the teacher: before class, read these Atlas entries: Straw Man, Ad Hominem.

Blind Spot Lab · Worksheet · What Is a Fallacy? Name: ................................

  1. Write down something you heard that was an ad hominem, then rewrite it to answer the actual point.

  2. Write one of your own opinions, then write a straw man of it: a twisted version of what you said.

  3. What's the difference between criticizing an idea and criticizing the person who holds it?

Lesson 2 · 45 minutes

How Do Ads Persuade Us?

Goal: Students spot persuasion tricks in ads: the single testimonial, the decoy option, and fake urgency.

1. Warm-up (5 minutes)

Show an ad from TV or social media (without naming the brand) and ask: "Which line made you want to buy it? Did it give you information, or just a feeling?"

2. Play (20 minutes)

Spot the Trick

3. Discuss (10 minutes)

  • Why isn't one "before and after" photo proof?
  • How does a super-expensive option make the other option look like a steal?

4. Worksheet (10 minutes)

For the teacher: before class, read these Atlas entries: Anecdotal Evidence, Decoy Effect.

Blind Spot Lab · Worksheet · How Do Ads Persuade Us? Name: ................................

  1. Write an ad for a made-up product with no tricks at all.

  2. Write the same ad using two tricks, and name each one.

  3. Which ad would sell more? Why?

Lesson 3 · 45 minutes

Is This Story Real?

Goal: Students use 5 fact-checking questions before believing or sharing a story: the source, the date, the image origin, the full quote, and other sources.

1. Warm-up (5 minutes)

Ask: "Has anyone ever shared something that turned out to be false? How did that feel?"

2. Play (20 minutes)

News Detective Rumor Factory

3. Discuss (10 minutes)

  • Why do stories that scare us or make us angry spread faster?
  • What's the difference between a fake story and a story that's missing context?

4. Worksheet (10 minutes)

For the teacher: before class, read these Atlas entries: Appeal to Emotion, Appeal to Authority.

Blind Spot Lab · Worksheet · Is This Story Real? Name: ................................

  1. Write the 5 questions you'll ask about any story before you share it.

  2. Pick one trick from Rumor Factory and explain how to spot it.

  3. Write a polite reply to a relative who posted a rumor in the family group chat.

Lesson 4 · 45 minutes

Do Numbers Lie?

Goal: Students read charts correctly and catch the cut-off axis, the cherry-picked time window, and totals that were never divided by population.

1. Warm-up (5 minutes)

Draw two bars on the board, 50 and 52, with the axis starting at 49. Ask: "Is that a big difference?" Then redraw them starting from zero.

2. Play (20 minutes)

The Lying Chart The Probability Tree

3. Discuss (10 minutes)

  • Why would someone make a misleading chart without writing a single wrong number?
  • "This city has the most car crashes": what else do you need to know before you judge?

4. Worksheet (10 minutes)

For the teacher: before class, read these Atlas entries: Cherry Picking, False Cause (Post Hoc).

Blind Spot Lab · Worksheet · Do Numbers Lie? Name: ................................

  1. Draw the same numbers twice: once honestly, once with a trick.

  2. Write a misleading headline for your second chart.

  3. Why do we sometimes need to divide by population?

Lesson 5 · 45 minutes

Our Brains Fool Us

Goal: Students learn that biases aren't stupidity but mental shortcuts, and get to know confirmation bias and anchoring.

1. Warm-up (5 minutes)

Give half the class a slip that says: "Are there more or fewer than 15 countries in Africa?" Give the other half: "More or fewer than 75?" Then everyone writes down their own estimate without looking at anyone else's, and you compare the two averages. (Answer: 54 UN member states.)

2. Play (20 minutes)

The Bias Lab Your Thinking Profile

3. Discuss (10 minutes)

  • Why do we believe news that agrees with us faster?
  • How can you protect yourself from the first number you hear?

4. Worksheet (10 minutes)

For the teacher: before class, read these Atlas entries: Confirmation Bias, Anchoring.

Blind Spot Lab · Worksheet · Our Brains Fool Us Name: ................................

  1. Describe a time you experienced confirmation bias.

  2. Pick a bias from "Your Thinking Profile" and write a small plan to reduce it.

  3. Has this bias ever helped you?

Lesson 6 · 45 minutes

Disagree Without Fighting

Goal: Students respond to the argument itself and practice acknowledging the strong points on the other side.

1. Warm-up (5 minutes)

Pick a lighthearted topic (summer or winter?) and split the class into two teams. Anyone who attacks a person instead of an idea costs their team a point.

2. Play (20 minutes)

The Family Group Chat Debate Dot

3. Discuss (10 minutes)

  • Why can a calm reply be more convincing than an aggressive one?
  • When is staying quiet the right call, and when does it let a mistake slide?

4. Worksheet (10 minutes)

For the teacher: before class, read these Atlas entries: Whataboutism, False Dilemma.

Blind Spot Lab · Worksheet · Disagree Without Fighting Name: ................................

  1. Write the opposite of your opinion on some topic, using the strongest argument you can.

  2. Write a reply to that argument without any fallacies.

  3. Write a sentence that acknowledges a good point the other side made.
