Money Decisions

Money isn't just math. Your brain brings its biases and feelings to every money decision. In 7 days you'll learn 7 traps that drain your wallet without you noticing, from credit card rewards to “Pay in 4.” This is practice in thinking, not financial or investment advice.

Day 1Sunk Cost Fallacy

You've already put a lot into something, so you keep going even when it's losing, so the money “isn't wasted.” But what you paid is gone either way.

Today's task: Think of a subscription or anything else you keep paying for just because you've already paid so much. Ask: if I were starting from zero today, would I sign up?

Quick question: You paid for a concert ticket, and on the day of the show you feel awful. What's the clear-headed way to think about it?

Day 2Loss Aversion

Losing $500 hurts more than winning $500 feels good. That can make you cling to losing bets and shy away from good decisions, like panicking every time you check your 401(k) after a bad week.

Today's task: Today, when you turn something down because “I might lose,” write next to it: and what could I gain? Then compare them calmly.

Quick question: What does loss aversion mean?

Day 3Scarcity Effect

“Only 3 left in stock” and “deal ends in 1 hour” make things seem more valuable, because your brain values whatever is rare.

Today's task: If you see “limited quantity” today, ask: if this were available all year, would I still buy it?

Quick question: The product page says “Only 2 left!” What is it playing on?

Day 4Decoy Effect

Sometimes an option is put in the middle not to be sold, but to make the pricier option look like a deal.

Today's task: On the first price menu you see, mentally remove one option and see if your choice changes.

Quick question: Coffee: small $2.95, medium $4.75, large $4.95. Why is the medium probably there?

Day 5Gambler's Fallacy

“I've lost 5 times in a row, so I'm due for a win.” But each round is independent, and luck doesn't keep a ledger.

Today's task: If you hear someone say “I'm due” at a casino, a scratch-off, or a fantasy league, think: does the coin or the dice remember what happened before?

Quick question: A fair coin lands tails 5 times in a row. What are the odds of heads on the next flip?

Day 6Planning Fallacy

We almost always estimate that a project will take less time and money than it really does, even when we've been through it before. The kitchen remodel that will “be done in a month” is a classic.

Today's task: Take an upcoming expense or project, and instead of asking “What will it cost?”, ask: “What did something similar actually cost last time?”

Quick question: What's the best way to fight the planning fallacy?

Day 7Second-Order Thinking

The first result of a decision isn't the only one. Splitting a purchase into four “interest-free” payments feels easy this month, but what happens when you've stacked five of them? Always ask: “And then what?”

Today's task: Before any Buy Now, Pay Later purchase or new subscription, write down: what happens in a month? And in a year?

Quick question: What does second-order thinking ask?
