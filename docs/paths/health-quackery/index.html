Health and Hype

Detox teas, miracle supplements, and “the cure doctors don't want you to know about” are all over your feed. In 7 days you'll learn to spot the tricks wellness hucksters use. Important: this is practice in thinking, not medical advice. Make any decision about your health or treatment with a doctor, and never stop a medication without asking them first.

Day 1Appeal to Nature

“100% natural” doesn't mean safe. Some plants and mushrooms are natural and poisonous. And natural products can also interact with medications you take.

Today's task: Read the label of a product that says “natural” and look for what it says about safety or dosage. If you take any medication, ask a doctor or pharmacist before combining them.

Quick question: “This product is natural, so it has no side effects.” What's going on in that sentence?

Day 2Anecdotal Evidence

“My aunt tried it and got better” is a story, not a study. She may have gotten better for other reasons, and you'll never hear from the people it didn't help.

Today's task: If someone tells you today about a remedy that “worked like magic,” ask: and how many people tried it and got nothing?

Quick question: Why isn't a personal story enough to show that a treatment works?

Day 3Regression to the Mean

People try the unusual remedy when they feel their worst. And many aches get better on their own after they peak. So the improvement gets credited to the remedy, when it was coming anyway.

Today's task: Think of a time you tried something when you felt your worst and then improved. Could you have gotten better without it? You don't need the answer. The question is what matters.

Quick question: Someone drinks an herbal tonic on the worst day of a cold, and the next day feels better. Which possibility should we consider?

Day 4False Cause (Post Hoc)

Two things happening one after the other doesn't mean the first caused the second. “Ever since I started wearing this bracelet, my back feels better” could be a coincidence.

Today's task: Find a health ad or influencer post that says “after I started using it,” and ask: what else could explain the improvement?

Quick question: “Ever since I cut out that food, my headaches went away.” What's the first question to ask?

Day 5Authority Bias

Someone in a white coat in a video isn't proof on their own. Ask: are they a specialist in this exact topic? And do they profit from selling the product?

Today's task: If you see a “doctor” promoting a product today, ask both questions: what's their specialty? And do they profit from the sale?

Quick question: A “doctor” in an Instagram ad is selling a supplement. What's the most important question?

Day 6Falsifiability Test

If a product “cures everything,” and when it doesn't work it's because “you didn't use it right,” then the claim is built so it can never be proven wrong. That's a red flag.

Today's task: Take a health claim you've heard and ask: what result would make the person admit they were wrong? If there isn't one, be careful.

Quick question: The seller says: “If it didn't work, you just weren't committed enough.” What does that tell you?

Day 7Shifting the Burden of Proof

Whoever says “this blend cures diabetes” has to bring the evidence. It's not your job to prove it doesn't. Real treatments are tested in studies before they're sold.

Today's task: If someone says “Prove it doesn't work,” answer: “You're the one selling it. Show me the evidence.” And for anything involving your health, ask a doctor.

Quick question: Who has to prove that a new treatment works?
