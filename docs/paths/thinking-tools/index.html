Thinking Tools

Good scientists, engineers, and detectives carry a toolbox for thinking: simple tools that save them from a lot of trouble. In 7 days you'll pick up 7 tools and try each one on a real situation from your day.

Day 1Occam's Razor

Occam's razor: if there are two explanations, start with the simpler one that needs fewer assumptions. That doesn't mean it's always right. It means it's the first one to test. The power went out in your building? Probably a fault, not a plot by the neighbors.

Today's task: Today, when you catch yourself explaining something with a complicated story, write the simpler explanation next to it and count the assumptions each one needs.

Quick question: What does Occam's razor say?

Day 2Hanlon's Razor

Hanlon's razor: don't blame on bad intent what can be explained by oversight or carelessness. Your friend didn't answer your text? They're probably busy, not mad at you.

Today's task: The first time today you feel someone is “doing it on purpose” to bug you, write two explanations that have nothing to do with intent, and ask: which is more likely?

Quick question: A coworker forgot to send you the file. What does Hanlon's razor tell you?

Day 3Steelmanning

Before you answer a view, state it in its strongest form, one its holder would agree with. If you can answer that strong version, your answer is worth something.

Today's task: Pick a view you disagree with and write the 3 strongest arguments for it. Only then write your response.

Quick question: What's the goal of steelmanning?

Day 4Chesterton's Fence

Chesterton's fence: if you find a fence in the middle of a road and don't know why it's there, don't tear it down until you find out. There may be an important reason you can't see. The idea comes from a 1929 book by the English writer G. K. Chesterton.

Today's task: Find a rule at home or at work that seems pointless. Before you push to get rid of it, ask someone who's been around longer: why was it made in the first place?

Quick question: Before you scrap an old rule, what does Chesterton's fence advise?

Day 5Inversion

Instead of asking “How do I succeed?”, flip the question: “What would guarantee that I fail?” Then avoid it. Working backward sometimes shows you what you've been missing.

Today's task: Pick one of your goals and write down 5 things that would guarantee you fail at it. You may find you're doing a few of them right now.

Quick question: What does inversion make you ask?

Day 6Second-Order Thinking

The first result is obvious, but what comes after it? Raising your prices earns you more today, but your customers might go elsewhere next month. Ask “And then what?” twice.

Today's task: Take a decision you'll make today, write down its first result, and then the result that first result will lead to.

Quick question: “I'll stay up late and finish the work.” What does second-order thinking add?

Day 7Premortem

Before you start any project, imagine it's 6 months later and it failed completely, then ask: what happened? This exercise surfaces problems nobody would have dared to mention.

Today's task: Take an upcoming plan, like a wedding, a trip, or a project, and write “The plan failed because…” and finish it 5 times. Then come up with a fix for the most dangerous reason.

Quick question: In a “premortem,” what do you imagine?
