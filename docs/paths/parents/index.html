For Parents: Teach Your Kids to Think

Critical thinking can be learned early, through games and questions, not lectures. For 7 days, there's one simple activity a day to do with your kids at dinner or in the car. Adjust it to their age.

Day 1Appeal to Popularity

“All my friends have one” is one of the first arguments kids learn. It's a great chance to teach them that popular isn't always right.

Today's task: Play a game: each person names something “everybody does,” and the others decide: is it that way because it's right, or just because everyone does it?

Quick question: Your son says, “Everyone in my class has this phone.” Which response teaches him to think?

Day 2False Cause (Post Hoc)

Kids connect things fast: “I wore these socks and my team won, so they're my lucky socks.” That's a great doorway to understanding cause and effect.

Today's task: Ask your kids about something they think is “lucky,” and talk it through: if we wore different socks, would the team definitely lose? Why not?

Quick question: “Every time I wash the car, it rains.” What's the lesson here?

Day 3Hasty Generalization

“Boys are all annoying,” because one boy bothered your daughter. We often teach kids to generalize from one incident without meaning to.

Today's task: When your child generalizes today, calmly ask: “All of them? Or one? Do you know anyone who's different?” And have them count on their fingers.

Quick question: After a fight with one girl, your daughter says, “All the girls on the bus are mean.” What do you say?

Day 4Ad Hominem

“You're just a crybaby,” instead of answering what was said. Kids do it in their fights, and so do adults.

Today's task: At dinner, tell a story: a kid shares a great idea, but he isn't popular in class. Does that make the idea bad? Let everyone give their opinion.

Quick question: Her brother says, “Don't listen to him, he's the youngest.” What is that reply attacking?

Day 5Straw Man

“Mom wants me to never play at all!” when you said one hour. Blowing someone's words up into a worse version is a trick that starts early.

Today's task: Play “telephone” with a long sentence, then talk about it: how did the message change? And why does it matter to repeat what people said the way they said it?

Quick question: You tell your son, “Only one hour of games today,” and he says, “You want me to never play at all!” What's this called?

Day 6Steelmanning

The opposite of a straw man: stating the other person's view in its best form before you respond. It's a skill that teaches kids to listen before they fight.

Today's task: In any disagreement between siblings today, before anyone responds, they have to restate the other's view until the other says, “Yes, that's exactly what I mean.”

Quick question: What does “steelmanning” mean?

Day 7Bayesian Updating

The most important lesson: changing your mind when new evidence comes in isn't losing, it's smart. Kids learn this by watching their parents do it.

Today's task: Tell your kids about something you used to believe that turned out to be wrong, and how you changed your mind. Then ask: have you ever changed your mind about something?

Quick question: When is changing your mind a good thing?
