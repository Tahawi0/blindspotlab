Bias

Halo Effect

Letting one good trait, like looks or fame, make us assume everything else about a person is good too.

Where you'll meet it: Work and school Shopping and money

In real life

At a job interview
The candidate is polished and charming, so the interview breezes by and nobody really digs into their experience.
In ads
A beloved actor promotes a payday-loan app called QuickCashly, and people assume the app must be legit.
At school
The star of the basketball team gets a little extra leniency when the teacher grades his English essay.

How to spot it

Ask: am I judging this specific thing, or am I being swayed by some other trait that has nothing to do with it?

How to respond without a fight

Rate each quality on its own. Make a list: experience, price, quality, and give each a separate score.

Don't confuse it with: Appeal to Authority

Appeal to authority is a fallacy inside an argument. The halo effect is a bias in our impressions, and it happens even when nobody is arguing with you.

Strength of evidence

Strong

Test yourself

"The actor in this commercial seems so classy and has such a great voice. Brightwell Insurance must be a good company."

What's the trick here?

A nice quality in the actor got transferred to the company, even though the two have nothing to do with each other.

Sources

  • Nisbett, R. E., & Wilson, T. D. (1977). The halo effect: Evidence for unconscious alteration of judgments. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 35(4), 250–256.
