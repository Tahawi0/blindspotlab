Home and family (46)

  • Straw ManFallacy“Maybe we eat out a little less this month.” — “So you want us to sit at home and never have any fun?!”
  • Ad HominemFallacy“You’re going to lecture me about spending? You can’t even save $50!” — The actual question was whether this purchase is too much.
  • False DilemmaFallacy“Either you get into a top-ten college, or you’re going to end up with nothing.”
  • Appeal to PopularityFallacy“Every kid in her class got a smartphone at eight, so that must be the right age.”
  • Confirmation BiasBiasYou’re convinced your neighbor is rude, so you remember every time he didn’t wave back — and forget the time he jumped your car.
  • Appeal to AuthorityFallacy“Your uncle the engineer says that medication doesn’t work.” — Your uncle is an engineer, not a doctor.
  • Appeal to TraditionFallacy“We’ve always bundled babies up in heavy blankets, even in July. You think you know better than Grandma?”
  • Appeal to EmotionFallacy“If you really loved me, you’d buy me this phone.”
  • Appeal to NatureFallacy“These herbs are natural, so there’s no way they can hurt you.”
  • Tu QuoqueFallacyDad: “Smoking is bad for you.” Teen: “You smoke!” — Smoking is still bad for you.
  • Red HerringFallacy“Where did the whole paycheck go?” — “Well, why don’t you ever make the food I like?”
  • Slippery SlopeFallacy“If we let him stay up until 11 tonight, tomorrow it’ll be 3 a.m., then he’ll drop out of school, and his whole future is ruined.”
  • False Cause (Post Hoc)Fallacy“I wore this jersey and my team won, so it’s my lucky jersey.”
  • Begging the QuestionFallacy“I’m right because I don’t make mistakes.”
  • Loaded QuestionFallacy“Have you stopped lying to me?” — Say yes or no, and you’ve admitted you were lying.
  • EquivocationFallacy“The doctor said fast food is heavy, and this burger is tiny, so it’s fine.” — The doctor meant hard on your body, not heavy on a scale.
  • No True ScotsmanFallacy“A real man never asks for directions.” — “My dad asks all the time.” — “Then he’s not a real man, is he?”
  • Appeal to IgnoranceFallacy“Nobody’s proven this house isn’t haunted, so it must be haunted.”
  • Anecdotal EvidenceFallacy“Grandpa smoked his whole life and lived to 90, so smoking can’t be that bad.”
  • False AnalogyFallacy“Kids are like houseplants. Just leave them alone and they’ll grow.” — But kids need raising, not just water and sunlight.
  • Argument from Personal IncredulityFallacy“How can a plane that weighs hundreds of tons just float in the air? Something doesn’t add up.”
  • Genetic FallacyFallacy“That idea came from some tech bro in Silicon Valley, so it can’t be any good.”
  • Fallacy of Composition / DivisionFallacy“I love every one of these ingredients, so the dish is going to be delicious.”
  • Gambler's FallacyFallacy“We've had three girls, so the next one's definitely a boy.” — The odds are still roughly 50/50.
  • False CompromiseFallacy“Your cousin says the Earth is round, your uncle says it's flat. The truth is probably somewhere in the middle.”
  • WhataboutismFallacy“Why is your room such a mess?” — “What about my brother's room? It's way worse!”
  • Fallacy FallacyFallacy“You told me not to smoke because Uncle Mike said so. That's an appeal to authority, so smoking must be fine.”
  • Availability HeuristicBiasEvery day there's a new post about a stolen package, so it feels like the whole area has gotten unsafe, even if the numbers say otherwise.
  • Hindsight BiasBias“It was obvious that marriage wasn't going to last.” — Said after the divorce.
  • Fundamental Attribution ErrorBiasYour kid didn't study: “He's just lazy.” Nobody asks whether he was sick or something was bothering him.
  • Status Quo BiasBiasYour phone plan is overpriced and doesn't fit how you use it, but you keep it because you're “used to it.”
  • Negativity BiasBiasOne harsh word in an argument stays with you for years, while plenty of kind words fade.
  • Authority BiasBiasSomeone in a hi-vis vest with a clipboard asks to come in and “check the meter,” and you let him in without asking who he's with.
  • Choice-Supportive BiasBiasYou picked a school for your kid, and now you can't stand to hear any criticism of it.
  • Moving the GoalpostsFallacy“Get straight A's and you can have a new phone.” — He gets straight A's. — “No, that was just the first semester. Wait till the end of the year.”
  • False EquivalenceFallacy“You forgot to pick up milk, and I forgot to pay the rent. So we're even.”
  • Special PleadingFallacy“No phones at dinner.” — A minute later, Dad takes a call: “That's work, it's different.”
  • Base Rate NeglectBias“Most car crashes happen close to home, so your own neighborhood is the real danger!” — Well, most of your driving happens close to home in the first place.
  • Planning FallacyBias“The kitchen remodel will take two months, done way before Thanksgiving.” Six months later, it’s still going and the budget has doubled.
  • GroupthinkBiasThe whole family is excited about your sister’s new fiancé. Everyone has a concern about him, but nobody wants to be “the one who ruins it.”
  • Endowment EffectBiasYour closet is full of clothes you haven’t worn in years, but every time you go to donate them, they feel “still perfectly good.”
  • IKEA EffectBiasYou cooked a new recipe for the first time, it came out just okay, and you can’t figure out why the family isn’t as excited as you are.
  • Peak–End RuleBiasThe day at the beach was crowded and hot the whole time, but it ended with ice cream on the boardwalk, so on the drive home everyone says, “What a great day.”
  • Curse of KnowledgeBiasYou’re showing your dad something on his phone: “Tap here, then here,” way too fast, and you get frustrated that he isn’t following.
  • Illusion of ControlBiasYou press the elevator button five times in a row, as if that will make it come faster.
  • False Consensus EffectBias“Who even eats this without hot sauce? Everybody likes it spicy.” And you’re the only one in the house who does.

Work and school (48)

  • Ad HominemFallacy“That idea came from the new hire, so obviously they don’t get how things work here.”
  • AnchoringBiasWhatever salary number gets said first in a negotiation shapes the whole conversation after it.
  • Appeal to TraditionFallacy“We’ve done this spreadsheet by hand for ten years. Why change it now?”
  • Tu QuoqueFallacy“You’re telling me not to be late? You were late yesterday.”
  • Red HerringFallacyThe manager asks why the project is behind, and the employee starts talking about how swamped the other department is.
  • Slippery SlopeFallacy“If we allow one work-from-home day, nobody will ever work again and the company will go under.”
  • False Cause (Post Hoc)Fallacy“Sales went up after we changed the logo color.” — Maybe it was the holiday season, or a promotion.
  • Hasty GeneralizationFallacy“The new hire made one mistake in a report. Guess they just can’t do the job.”
  • Begging the QuestionFallacy“This plan is the best one, because there’s no plan better than it.”
  • Loaded QuestionFallacy“When are you going to stop holding up the project?”
  • Shifting the Burden of ProofFallacy“That guy is leaking info to competitors, and if you don’t believe me, prove he isn’t.”
  • Appeal to IgnoranceFallacy“Nobody has complained about the new system, so everyone must love it.” — Maybe people just don’t know where to complain.
  • Anecdotal EvidenceFallacy“I hired one person from that college and they were terrible, so none of their grads are any good.”
  • Cherry PickingFallacyA report highlights the one month when sales were high and skips the months when they dropped.
  • False AnalogyFallacy“We’re a family here, so a good employee doesn’t complain about unpaid overtime.”
  • Argument from Personal IncredulityFallacy“No way this software does the whole job in a minute. They’re fudging the numbers.”
  • Genetic FallacyFallacy“Our competitor came up with that process, so we’re not using it.”
  • Fallacy of Composition / DivisionFallacy“That company is hugely successful, so every single person who works there must be brilliant.”
  • False CompromiseFallacy“The engineer says the bridge needs 100 tons of steel, the contractor says 50. Let's split it at 75.” — 75 might not hold the bridge up.
  • Sunk Cost FallacyFallacy“We've spent a year on this project. We can't stop now.” — Even when it's clearly not going to work.
  • Availability HeuristicBiasAt review time, the manager remembers your most recent slip-up and forgets a full year of solid work.
  • Halo EffectBiasThe candidate is polished and charming, so the interview breezes by and nobody really digs into their experience.
  • Hindsight BiasBias“That project was clearly doomed.” — Nobody said so before it failed.
  • Fundamental Attribution ErrorBiasYour coworker joins the meeting late: “So unprofessional.” You join late: “Traffic was brutal.”
  • In-Group BiasBiasThe manager keeps handing opportunities to people from his alma mater without realizing it.
  • Status Quo BiasBiasA new tool would cut the team's busywork in half, but nobody wants to try it.
  • Optimism BiasBias“This project will take a week.” — And it takes a month, like every time.
  • Negativity BiasBiasTen customers thanked you today and one complained, and on the drive home all you can think about is the complaint.
  • Survivorship BiasBias“That founder dropped out of college and became a billionaire, so college is pointless.” — Nobody talks about the thousands who dropped out and didn't make it.
  • Spotlight EffectBiasYou stumbled over one sentence in your presentation and replayed it for a week. Nobody else remembered.
  • Authority BiasBiasThe boss pitches a bad idea in the meeting, and nobody pushes back.
  • OverconfidenceBias“I'm 100% sure that number is right.” — Then someone double-checks, and it's wrong.
  • Dunning–Kruger EffectBiasA new hire thinks the job is easy and the veterans just don't get it, until they dig in a little deeper.
  • Choice-Supportive BiasBiasYou left one job for another, and now you only remember the bad parts of the old one, even things that never bothered you.
  • Moving the GoalpostsFallacy“If the app gets 100 sign-ups in a month, we keep funding it.” — It gets 120. — “No, we really needed 500 for it to count as a success.”
  • Special PleadingFallacyThe manager docks pay for any lateness, but when he's late: “My situation is different. I have responsibilities.”
  • Regression to the MeanBiasA manager chews out an employee after his worst month ever, and the next month is better. So the manager concludes that yelling gets results.
  • Planning FallacyBias“I’ll get through all the reading in the two days before the final.” The night before the exam, you realize you’re only halfway.
  • Barnum EffectBiasA team offsite runs a “personality test,” and the result says: “You love people, but you also need time to recharge.” Everyone in the room feels seen.
  • GroupthinkBiasThe boss pitches an idea in the meeting and everyone says “Love it.” Afterward, people DM each other on Slack: “That idea is going to flop.”
  • Self-Serving BiasBias“I aced it because I studied hard.” And in the class you failed: “The professor is unfair and the exam was impossible.”
  • Nirvana FallacyFallacy“The new system still has bugs, so let’s go back to paper.” And paper has way more problems.
  • IKEA EffectBiasYou defend the idea you sweated over with everything you’ve got in the meeting, even when a coworker’s idea is simpler and better.
  • Mere-Exposure EffectBiasA new coworker you weren’t sure about, and after two months of sitting next to them you actually like them, without anything special happening.
  • Peak–End RuleBiasA customer waited on hold for half an hour, but the rep solved the problem kindly at the end, so they gave the service five stars.
  • Curse of KnowledgeBiasThe teacher says “This part is obvious” and skips three steps in the problem, and the whole class is lost.
  • Just-World BeliefBiasA coworker is let go in a round of layoffs, and you hear: “They must have been slacking on something.” Even though the cut hit the whole department.
  • False Consensus EffectBias“I love working late, so I’m sure the whole team is fine with a 9 p.m. call.” Nobody was asked.

Online and in the news (43)

  • Straw ManFallacySomeone posts that e-scooters need clearer parking rules downtown. Top reply: “So you want to ban scooters and make everyone drive?”
  • Ad HominemFallacy“Ignore him, he’s a loser who can’t even hold a job.” — posted as a reply to a thread full of numbers and sources.
  • False DilemmaFallacy“You’re either with us, or you’re a traitor.”
  • Appeal to PopularityFallacy“This post has 100,000 likes, so what it says has to be true.”
  • Confirmation BiasBiasYou only follow accounts that agree with you, so it feels like “everyone” sees things your way.
  • Appeal to AuthorityFallacy“That actor says this coin is going to the moon, so I have to buy in.”
  • Appeal to EmotionFallacy“Share if you have a heart. Anyone who scrolls past is cold as ice.”
  • Appeal to NatureFallacyA wellness influencer: “Ditch your meds and try this natural remedy instead.”
  • Tu QuoqueFallacy“You’re posting about littering when I literally saw you toss a cup out your car window once.”
  • Red HerringFallacyA reporter asks about the service outage, and the spokesperson gives a long speech about the company’s awards from years ago.
  • False Cause (Post Hoc)Fallacy“Ever since they put up that cell tower, people around here keep getting sick. It has to be the tower.”
  • Hasty GeneralizationFallacy“I met two people from Boston and they were both rude. Everyone from Boston is like that.”
  • Begging the QuestionFallacy“This page is trustworthy, because it says it only posts the truth.”
  • Loaded QuestionFallacy“Why is the city ignoring the pothole problem?” — before anyone has shown that it is.
  • EquivocationFallacy“Germ theory? It’s just a theory, and a theory is just a guess.” — In science, a “theory” is an explanation backed by a large body of evidence, not a guess.
  • No True ScotsmanFallacy“Midwesterners are all nice.” — “My neighbor from Ohio is a jerk.” — “Well, he’s not a real Midwesterner.”
  • Shifting the Burden of ProofFallacy“This supplement cures everything, and if you don’t believe me, prove it doesn’t.”
  • Appeal to IgnoranceFallacy“Scientists haven’t been able to prove this remedy doesn’t work, so it works.”
  • Anecdotal EvidenceFallacy“My cousin tried this cream and her skin cleared up, so it’s the best cream out there.”
  • Cherry PickingFallacyA restaurant’s website shows three glowing reviews and leaves out the hundred one-star ones.
  • False AnalogyFallacy“Running a city is just like running your household budget — if you’re in debt, you sell the furniture.” — A city’s finances don’t work like a family’s checking account.
  • Argument from Personal IncredulityFallacy“There’s no way my phone knows where I am with GPS turned off. That’s a lie.”
  • Genetic FallacyFallacy“That was reported by a news site I can’t stand, so it’s obviously fake.”
  • False CompromiseFallacyA segment seats a doctor next to a wellness influencer and presents their views on vaccines as two equally supported sides.
  • WhataboutismFallacy“This company's customer service is awful.” — “What about the other company? They're even worse!”
  • Fallacy FallacyFallacy“This ad is pure emotional manipulation, so the product has to be junk.”
  • Bandwagon EffectBiasA new trend takes off, everyone's doing it, so you do it too without asking whether it actually fits you.
  • In-Group BiasBiasBad news about someone from your hometown: “Must be fake.” The same news about someone from the rival town across the river: “Typical.”
  • Negativity BiasBiasBad news tends to spread faster and get more engagement than good news.
  • Survivorship BiasBiasVideos titled “How I made a million day trading.” The people who lost money don't make videos.
  • Spotlight EffectBiasYou hold off on posting because “what will people think,” when most of them will scroll past in a second.
  • Authority BiasBiasA wellness account puts “Dr.” in front of its name, and people trust its health tips, even though the degree isn't in medicine.
  • Dunning–Kruger EffectBiasSomeone watched two YouTube videos about economics and now argues with economists in the comments, totally confident.
  • Moving the GoalpostsFallacy“Show me proof.” — Here's a study. — “That one's tiny.” — Here's a big one. — “Somebody must have paid for it.”
  • False EquivalenceFallacyA segment treats a long wait at an urgent care clinic and a ring selling counterfeit pills as “the same problem.”
  • Barnum EffectBias“What your favorite color says about you”: “You’re kind, but nobody really gets you.” So you share it and write, “This is literally me!”
  • Scarcity EffectBiasAn ad says: “24 hours only!” A week later, the same deal is still running.
  • GroupthinkBiasThe friend group chat plans a Vegas trip. Anyone who asks about the budget gets “Why are you always such a buzzkill?” So they stop asking.
  • Texas Sharpshooter FallacyFallacyA video: “The numbers in your birthday hold a secret!” It collects every number that matches and stays quiet about every one that doesn’t.
  • Endowment EffectBiasYou list your used phone on Facebook Marketplace and get annoyed when someone haggles. Yet you haggle on every used phone you buy.
  • IKEA EffectBiasYou spent three days shooting and editing a video, and you don’t get why it’s doing worse than one you threw together in 15 minutes.
  • Mere-Exposure EffectBiasA creator you didn’t know or care about shows up in your feed twenty times, and now you watch their videos to the end.
  • Just-World BeliefBiasA post about someone who got scammed, and the top comments are: “Serves them right for being greedy.” Before anyone even asks how the scam worked.

Shopping and money (29)

  • False DilemmaFallacy“Buy today at this price, or regret it for the rest of your life.”
  • Appeal to PopularityFallacy“Over a million Americans have switched!” — The number doesn’t tell you whether it’s right for you.
  • Confirmation BiasBiasYou’ve already decided on a certain car, so you read only the glowing reviews and wave off the bad ones as “probably fake.”
  • AnchoringBiasThe seller says, “It’s $80, but for you, $50,” and you feel like you scored. It might really be worth $30.
  • Appeal to AuthorityFallacyA famous quarterback tells you this shampoo makes your hair stronger.
  • Appeal to EmotionFallacyA commercial packed with sad piano music and teary kids that ends with: “Upgrade your phone plan today.”
  • Appeal to NatureFallacy“100% natural” printed on a bottle with zero information about what it actually does.
  • Hasty GeneralizationFallacy“We ordered from that store once and it broke in a week. Everything they sell is junk.”
  • EquivocationFallacy“Light” on the label — light in weight? In calories? In color? In flavor?
  • Shifting the Burden of ProofFallacy“This car has a problem.” — “What problem?” — “You prove to me it doesn’t.”
  • Cherry PickingFallacy“9 out of 10 customers are satisfied!” — without saying how many they asked or how they chose them.
  • Gambler's FallacyFallacy“This number hasn't come up in months. It's got to hit soon.”
  • Sunk Cost FallacyFallacy“I've already sunk $5,000 into repairs on this car, so I have to fix it again.” — Even if the repair now costs more than the car is worth.
  • Halo EffectBiasA beloved actor promotes a payday-loan app called QuickCashly, and people assume the app must be legit.
  • Bandwagon EffectBiasYou see a long line outside a store and join it before you even know what they're selling.
  • Loss AversionBias“Don't miss out on this deal” tends to hit harder than “Get a discount,” even though they mean the same thing.
  • Framing EffectBiasGround beef labeled “95% lean” sells better than the same beef labeled “5% fat.”
  • Status Quo BiasBiasYour savings have sat for years in an account that pays next to nothing, because switching feels like a hassle.
  • Survivorship BiasBias“Old furniture was built to last, not like today.” — The old stuff that fell apart got thrown out. Only the good pieces are left.
  • Decoy EffectBiasBasic is $10, Premium is $15, and “Standard Plus” is $14.50 with fewer features than Premium. The third plan is the decoy.
  • OverconfidenceBias“I really understand the market.” — So he puts his entire 401(k) into a single stock.
  • Choice-Supportive BiasBiasOnce you drive it off the lot, you notice every feature you love, and any flaw becomes “no big deal.”
  • Scarcity EffectBias“Only 3 left in stock!” So you bought a jacket you weren’t even planning to get.
  • Appeal to ConsequencesFallacy“If that crypto platform turns out to be a scam, I’ve lost my life savings. So there’s no way it’s a scam.”
  • Nirvana FallacyFallacy“If I can’t save $500 a month, I’m not going to save at all.”
  • Endowment EffectBias“My car is worth $18,000.” Every listing and trade-in offer says about $14,500, but you’re sure they “just don’t get it.”
  • Mere-Exposure EffectBiasA commercial jingle you never really noticed is suddenly stuck in your head a month later, and at the store your hand goes straight to that brand.
  • Illusion of ControlBias“I’ll pick my own lottery numbers instead of a Quick Pick. That way my odds are better.” The drawing is random either way.
  • False Consensus EffectBiasYou made an ad that fits your own taste and figured “Everyone will love it like I do.” Then the engagement came in weak.

Sports and out and about (26)

  • Straw ManFallacyA caller says the team should rest its star player more. The host fires back: “Oh, so you want him sitting on the bench all season?”
  • Appeal to TraditionFallacy“A wedding has to be done this way. That’s just how people do it.”
  • No True ScotsmanFallacy“No true fan ever boos their own team.” — “Plenty of fans boo.” — “Those aren’t real fans.”
  • Fallacy of Composition / DivisionFallacy“We just signed five All-Stars, so we’re guaranteed to be the best team in the league.” — A team of stars might not play well together.
  • Gambler's FallacyFallacy“This ref has given us a bunch of penalties lately, so the next close call has to go our way.”
  • Sunk Cost FallacyFallacy“We paid $16 a ticket. We're sitting through this terrible movie to the end.”
  • WhataboutismFallacy“That was a dirty hit by our guy.” — “What about their linebacker last week?”
  • Availability HeuristicBiasAfter seeing news coverage of a plane crash, you feel more nervous about flying than driving, even though the drive is far riskier.
  • Loss AversionBiasLosing a $20 bill bugs you a lot more than finding a $20 bill on the sidewalk makes you happy.
  • Hindsight BiasBiasAfter the game: “I knew they'd lose the second I saw the starting lineup.”
  • Fundamental Attribution ErrorBiasThe car ahead cuts you off: “What a reckless jerk.” When you cut someone off: “I was running late.”
  • In-Group BiasBiasWhen your team's player makes a hard hit, it's “playing tough.” When the other team does it, it's “dirty.”
  • Optimism BiasBias“I'm a good driver, I'm not going to crash.” — So you text while driving.
  • Spotlight EffectBiasThere's a small coffee stain on your shirt, and you feel like everyone's staring at it all day.
  • Decoy EffectBiasSmall popcorn is $5, medium is $8.50, large is $9. The medium is there to make the large look like a steal.
  • OverconfidenceBiasIn surveys, most drivers rate themselves as more skilled than most other drivers, which can't be true for most of us.
  • False EquivalenceFallacy“The guy who showed up five minutes late to practice is the same as the guy who shoved the ref. They both broke the rules.”
  • Special PleadingFallacyA driver leans on the horn at someone double-parked, then double-parks in the same spot himself: “It's just for a minute.”
  • Regression to the MeanBiasA rookie running back rushes for 200 yards one Sunday, then 40 the next. “The fame got to his head.” Or that first game was just a freak outlier.
  • Self-Serving BiasBiasWhen your team wins: “We played great.” When it loses: “The refs robbed us.”
  • Appeal to ConsequencesFallacy“If that catch gets overturned, we’re out of the playoffs. So it was obviously a catch.”
  • Texas Sharpshooter FallacyFallacyAfter the season ends, a fan digs through the team’s results until he finds they won every game played on a Thursday, and posts: “Thursday is our day. Not a coincidence.” He had no prediction about Thursdays before he went looking.
  • Nirvana FallacyFallacy“Seatbelts don’t save everyone in a crash, so what’s the point?”
  • Peak–End RuleBiasThe team looked awful for eight innings, then hit a walk-off home run, and everyone leaves the stadium saying, “What a game.”
  • Illusion of ControlBiasYou sit in the same spot on the couch and wear the same jersey every game so your team will win.
  • Just-World BeliefBiasSomeone gets their phone snatched on the subway, and instead of helping, people say: “Well, why did you have it out in a crowd?”

Health and food (7)

  • Framing EffectBias“This surgery has a 90% success rate” sounds more reassuring than “10% of these surgeries fail,” even though it's the same number.
  • Optimism BiasBias“That stuff happens to other people.” — So you keep putting off your annual checkup.
  • Dunning–Kruger EffectBiasThat famous chart with “Mount Stupid” and the “Valley of Despair” doesn't appear anywhere in the original study.
  • Base Rate NeglectBiasA test always catches the disease if you have it, but it also comes back falsely “positive” for 5% of healthy people. You test positive for a rare disease that affects 1 in 1,000 people, and you panic. But picture 1,000 people: 1 is actually sick, and about 50 healthy people also test positive. So most positive results here are false alarms. (This is an illustration, not medical advice. Ask your doctor what your own results mean.)
  • Regression to the MeanBias“My headache was at its absolute worst, I tried the tea a wellness influencer swears by, and it went away.” The headache was probably going to ease up on its own.
  • Appeal to ConsequencesFallacy“If the test shows something, it’d be a disaster, so there’s definitely nothing wrong.” And so they never get the test done.
  • Texas Sharpshooter FallacyFallacy“Three people on our street have the same illness. It must be the cell tower on that roof.” Nobody checked whether that number is actually unusual for how many people live there.
