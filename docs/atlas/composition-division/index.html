Fallacy of Composition / Division

Composition / Division

You assume that what’s true of the parts is true of the whole, or the other way around.

Where you'll meet it: Home and family Work and school Sports and out and about

In real life

In sports
“We just signed five All-Stars, so we’re guaranteed to be the best team in the league.” — A team of stars might not play well together.
In the kitchen
“I love every one of these ingredients, so the dish is going to be delicious.”
At work
“That company is hugely successful, so every single person who works there must be brilliant.”

How to spot it

Ask: does this quality really carry over from the part to the whole, or from the whole to the part? Or is the whole something different from the sum of its parts?

How to respond without a fight

“Each piece is great on its own, but that’s no guarantee they’ll be great together.”

Don't confuse it with: Hasty Generalization

A hasty generalization judges many individuals from a few examples. Composition and division confuse the traits of the parts with the traits of the whole thing itself.

Test yourself

"Every player on this all-star roster is a starter on a top team. They're definitely winning the championship."

What's the trick here?

Great individual players don't guarantee a team that plays well together.

Sources

  • Copi, I., Cohen, C., & McMahon, K. (2014). Introduction to Logic. Pearson.
