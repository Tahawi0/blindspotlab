Bias

Loss Aversion

The sting of losing something tends to feel bigger than the joy of gaining something of the same value.

Where you'll meet it: Shopping and money Sports and out and about

In real life

In everyday life
Losing a $20 bill bugs you a lot more than finding a $20 bill on the sidewalk makes you happy.
In ads
“Don't miss out on this deal” tends to hit harder than “Get a discount,” even though they mean the same thing.
In investing
A stock keeps dropping, and you won't sell because that would mean admitting the loss, so you lose even more.

How to spot it

Ask: am I rejecting this because it's actually a bad decision, or because I don't want to feel the loss?

How to respond without a fight

Look at the whole decision: “If I didn't already own this, would I buy it today at this price?”

Don't confuse it with: Sunk Cost Fallacy

Loss aversion is a general feeling that losses hurt more. The sunk cost fallacy is one result of it in a specific decision: continuing because of what you've already spent.

Strength of evidence

Strong, though recent research debates how big the effect really is

Test yourself

"This car burns through gas, but I'm not selling it now. I'd get $4,000 less than I paid."

What's the trick here?

The fear of feeling a loss outweighs thinking about future savings. The real question: what does keeping it cost you from today on?

Sources

  • Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory. Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291.
  • Gal, D., & Rucker, D. D. (2018). The loss of loss aversion. Journal of Consumer Psychology, 28(3), 497–516.
