Bias

Fundamental Attribution Error

When someone else messes up, we blame their character and overlook the circumstances. When we mess up, we see the circumstances right away.

Where you'll meet it: Home and family Work and school Sports and out and about

In real life

On the highway
The car ahead cuts you off: “What a reckless jerk.” When you cut someone off: “I was running late.”
On the office Slack
Your coworker joins the meeting late: “So unprofessional.” You join late: “Traffic was brutal.”
At home
Your kid didn't study: “He's just lazy.” Nobody asks whether he was sick or something was bothering him.

How to spot it

Ask: if I had done the exact same thing, how would I explain it?

How to respond without a fight

Before you judge someone, think of three circumstances that could be behind what they did.

Don't confuse it with: Ad Hominem

Ad hominem is a fallacy in a debate: attacking the person instead of their argument. The attribution error is a bias in how we judge people's behavior, even when no one is debating.

Strength of evidence

Moderate, and it varies across cultures

Test yourself

"The clerk at the DMV is slow because he just doesn't care." You don't know their system has been down since 8 a.m.

What's the trick here?

The delay got blamed on the person's character, and the situation around him was ignored.

Sources

  • Ross, L. (1977). The intuitive psychologist and his shortcomings. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 10, 173–220.
  • Choi, I., Nisbett, R. E., & Norenzayan, A. (1999). Causal attribution across cultures. Psychological Bulletin, 125(1), 47–63.
