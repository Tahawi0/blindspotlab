The Atlas

Every fallacy and bias, A to Z. Each entry has everyday examples, how to spot it, and how to answer it.

✨ See the Atlas as a night sky 🧭 Browse by situation 🛠 Thinking Tools

A
  • Ad HominemFallacyYou attack the person instead of answering what they said.
  • AnchoringBiasThe first number we hear pulls our judgment of every number after it, even when it has nothing to do with anything.
  • Anecdotal EvidenceFallacyYou treat one personal experience or story as stronger evidence than data and studies.
  • Appeal to AuthorityFallacyYou treat a claim as true because someone important or famous said it, even if they’re not an expert on that topic.
  • Appeal to ConsequencesFallacyJudging an idea true or false because its outcome would be good or bad, not because of the evidence.
  • Appeal to EmotionFallacyYou try to win someone over by stirring up their feelings instead of giving them a real reason.
  • Appeal to IgnoranceFallacyYou treat something as true because nobody has proven it false, or false because nobody has proven it true.
  • Appeal to NatureFallacyYou treat something as good or safe because it’s “natural,” or bad because it’s “artificial.”
  • Appeal to PopularityFallacyYou treat an idea as true because lots of people believe it or do it.
  • Appeal to TraditionFallacyYou treat something as right because “we’ve always done it this way.”
  • Argument from Personal IncredulityFallacyYou reject something because it’s hard for you to imagine or understand, not because there’s evidence it’s wrong.
  • Authority BiasBiasOur tendency to obey or believe anyone who looks authoritative, like a uniform, a title, or a position, without thinking it through.
  • Availability HeuristicBiasJudging how common or dangerous something is by how easily examples come to mind.
B
  • Bandwagon EffectBiasOur tendency to do or believe something because lots of people around us do.
  • Barnum EffectBiasWe believe a very general description is about us specifically, even though it fits almost anyone.
  • Base Rate NeglectBiasFocusing on the details in front of us and forgetting how common the thing is in the first place.
  • Bayesian UpdatingThinking ToolHold your view with a level of confidence, and raise or lower it based on how strong each new piece of evidence is, instead of all-or-nothing.
  • Begging the QuestionFallacyYou use the conclusion you’re trying to prove as evidence for itself, so you just go in a circle.
C
  • Cherry PickingFallacyYou show only the evidence that supports your side and hide the evidence against it.
  • Chesterton's FenceThinking ToolDon’t remove a rule or an old practice until you first know why it was put there.
  • Choice-Supportive BiasBiasAfter we choose something, we remember its upsides more and forget its downsides, to convince ourselves we chose right.
  • Confirmation BiasBiasWe look for information that backs up what we already believe, and ignore or downplay what goes against it.
  • Curse of KnowledgeBiasOnce you know something well, it’s hard to imagine someone else not knowing it, so you explain it as if they already do.
D
  • Decoy EffectBiasAdding a third option nobody would really pick can make one of the original two look much better.
  • Dunning–Kruger EffectBiasThe idea that people who know little about a subject overestimate their skill in it. But the evidence for the famous version of this idea is disputed.
E
  • Endowment EffectBiasWe value something more just because it’s ours, so we ask more for it than we’d ever pay to buy it.
  • EquivocationFallacyYou use the same word in two different senses within one argument to reach a false conclusion.
F
  • Fallacy FallacyFallacyRejecting an idea entirely because the argument for it contains a fallacy, even though the idea itself might be true.
  • Fallacy of Composition / DivisionFallacyYou assume that what’s true of the parts is true of the whole, or the other way around.
  • False AnalogyFallacyYou compare two things that are alike in one way and conclude they must be alike in another, unrelated way.
  • False Cause (Post Hoc)FallacyYou assume one thing caused another just because it happened before it, or alongside it.
  • False CompromiseFallacyAssuming the truth must sit halfway between two positions, even when one of them is flat-out wrong.
  • False Consensus EffectBiasWe think most people think and choose like we do, more than they actually do.
  • False DilemmaFallacyYou present just two options as if they’re the only ones, when there are plenty of others.
  • False EquivalenceFallacyTreating two things as equal because they share one feature, even though they differ hugely in size or seriousness.
  • Falsifiability TestThinking ToolDecide up front what evidence would make you admit you’re wrong.
  • First-Principles ThinkingThinking ToolBreak a problem down to the basic facts you’re sure of, and build the solution from there instead of copying “what everyone usually does.”
  • Framing EffectBiasThe exact same information can lead us to different decisions depending on how it's worded.
  • Fundamental Attribution ErrorBiasWhen someone else messes up, we blame their character and overlook the circumstances. When we mess up, we see the circumstances right away.
G
  • Gambler's FallacyFallacyBelieving a random event is “due” because it hasn't happened in a while, or won't happen because it just did.
  • Genetic FallacyFallacyYou judge an idea as right or wrong based on where it came from, not on what it actually says.
  • GroupthinkBiasA group cares more about staying in agreement than about getting the decision right, so nobody pushes back.
H
  • Halo EffectBiasLetting one good trait, like looks or fame, make us assume everything else about a person is good too.
  • Hanlon's RazorThinking ToolDon’t explain someone’s behavior with bad intent if a slip, a mistake, or simply not knowing is enough to explain it.
  • Hasty GeneralizationFallacyYou draw a conclusion about everything from just one or two examples.
  • Hindsight BiasBiasAfter something happens, we feel like we saw it coming all along, and that it was obvious.
I
  • IKEA EffectBiasWe see something as better and more valuable when we put effort into making it ourselves, even if it’s ordinary or worse than the store-bought version.
  • Illusion of ControlBiasWe think we can influence things that are actually up to chance or outside our control.
  • In-Group BiasBiasOur tendency to believe, back, and give more chances to people from our own group, like our hometown, team, school, or family.
  • InversionThinking ToolInstead of asking “What should I do to succeed?” ask “What would guarantee I fail?” and steer clear of it.
J
  • Just-World BeliefBiasWe want to believe people get what they deserve, so when something bad happens to someone, we tend to look for a mistake they made that explains it.
L
  • Loaded QuestionFallacyA question with an accusation or assumption built in, so any direct answer admits the assumption.
  • Loss AversionBiasThe sting of losing something tends to feel bigger than the joy of gaining something of the same value.
M
  • Mere-Exposure EffectBiasWe like something more just because we’ve seen or heard it a lot, even without paying attention to it.
  • Moving the GoalpostsFallacySetting a condition for being convinced, then changing it to something harder once it's met, instead of admitting the point.
N
  • Negativity BiasBiasBad things affect us more and stick in memory longer than good things of the same size.
  • Nirvana FallacyFallacyRejecting a good solution because it isn’t perfect, by comparing it to an ideal solution that doesn’t exist.
  • No True ScotsmanFallacyWhen an example breaks your claim, you redefine the group to push that example out, instead of admitting the claim was too broad.
O
  • Occam's RazorThinking ToolIf you have more than one explanation for the same thing, start with the one that needs the fewest assumptions.
  • Optimism BiasBiasBelieving bad things are less likely to happen to us than to other people.
  • OverconfidenceBiasBeing more sure of what we know and what we estimate than our actual accuracy justifies.
P
  • Peak–End RuleBiasWe remember an experience by its most intense moment and by how it ended, not by the whole thing or how long it lasted.
  • Planning FallacyBiasWe underestimate how much time and money our own projects will take, even when every past project took longer.
  • PremortemThinking ToolBefore you start a plan, imagine it has already failed, and ask: “Why did it fail?”
R
  • Red HerringFallacyYou switch to a different topic to get away from the original question.
  • Regression to the MeanBiasAfter any really extreme result, the next one is usually closer to normal. When we forget that, we invent a reason for the change.
S
  • Scarcity EffectBiasWe see something as more valuable and more desirable just because it’s rare or the deal is about to end.
  • Second-Order ThinkingThinking ToolDon’t stop at the first consequence of a decision. Ask: “And then what?”
  • Self-Serving BiasBiasWhen we succeed, it’s because we’re good. When we fail, it’s because of the circumstances or other people.
  • Shifting the Burden of ProofFallacyYou make a claim, then demand that others prove it wrong instead of proving it right yourself.
  • Slippery SlopeFallacyYou claim one small step will inevitably lead to disaster, without showing that each step will actually cause the next.
  • Special PleadingFallacyApplying a rule to everyone else while exempting yourself or your group without a convincing reason.
  • Spotlight EffectBiasBelieving other people notice our appearance and our mistakes far more than they actually do.
  • Status Quo BiasBiasPreferring that things stay the way they are, even when a change would be better for us.
  • SteelmanningThinking ToolPresent the other person’s view in its strongest, best form, and only then respond to it.
  • Straw ManFallacyYou twist what the other person said, then argue against the twisted version instead of the real one.
  • Sunk Cost FallacyFallacySticking with a losing bet because of what you've already put in, not because it's worth continuing.
  • Survivorship BiasBiasLooking only at the people or things that made it, and forgetting the ones that tried the same thing and failed, because they're no longer visible.
T
  • Texas Sharpshooter FallacyFallacyPainting the target after you shoot: grabbing a slice of data that happens to show a pattern, ignoring the rest, and saying “See? Not a coincidence.”
  • Tu QuoqueFallacyYou dismiss advice or criticism because the person giving it makes the same mistake.
W
  • WhataboutismFallacyAnswering a criticism by pointing at someone else's wrongdoing instead of addressing the criticism itself.
