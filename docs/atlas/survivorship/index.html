Bias

Survivorship Bias

Looking only at the people or things that made it, and forgetting the ones that tried the same thing and failed, because they're no longer visible.

Where you'll meet it: Work and school Online and in the news Shopping and money

In real life

In business
“That founder dropped out of college and became a billionaire, so college is pointless.” — Nobody talks about the thousands who dropped out and didn't make it.
At the antique store
“Old furniture was built to last, not like today.” — The old stuff that fell apart got thrown out. Only the good pieces are left.
On YouTube
Videos titled “How I made a million day trading.” The people who lost money don't make videos.

How to spot it

Ask: where are the people who tried the same thing and failed? And how many of them are there?

How to respond without a fight

“Okay, that's a success story. But out of every 100 people who tried this, how many made it?”

Don't confuse it with: Cherry Picking

Cherry picking is when someone chooses to hide the evidence against them. In survivorship bias, the failures vanish from view on their own, with no one hiding them.

Strength of evidence

Strong, since it's a well-established statistical error

Test yourself

"Look at all these famous YouTubers. They quit their jobs and live off their channels. Let's quit ours."

What's the trick here?

You're only seeing the ones who made it. Thousands quit their jobs and their channels went nowhere, and nobody sees them.

Sources

  • Wald, A. (1943). A method of estimating plane vulnerability based on damage of survivors. Statistical Research Group, Columbia University.
  • Brown, S. J., Goetzmann, W., Ibbotson, R. G., & Ross, S. A. (1992). Survivorship bias in performance studies. Review of Financial Studies, 5(4), 553–580.
