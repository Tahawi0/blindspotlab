Bias

Curse of Knowledge

Once you know something well, it’s hard to imagine someone else not knowing it, so you explain it as if they already do.

Where you'll meet it: Home and family Work and school

In real life

At school
The teacher says “This part is obvious” and skips three steps in the problem, and the whole class is lost.
At work
You send a client an email full of phrases like “Q3 roadmap,” “KPIs,” and “circling back on the deck,” and they have no idea what you want from them.
At home
You’re showing your dad something on his phone: “Tap here, then here,” way too fast, and you get frustrated that he isn’t following.

How to spot it

Ask: if I didn’t know this yet, would I understand what I just said? If there’s a word an ordinary person wouldn’t know, the curse of knowledge is probably at work.

How to respond without a fight

Explain it to someone outside the topic and ask them to say it back in their own words. And use an everyday example instead of the technical term.

Don't confuse it with: False Consensus Effect

The false consensus effect is thinking people think and choose like you. The curse of knowledge is thinking they know what you know. The first is about opinions; the second is about information.

Strength of evidence

Moderate — the finding has replicated, but its size depends on the kind of information and the situation

Sources

  • Camerer, C., Loewenstein, G., & Weber, M. (1989). The curse of knowledge in economic settings: An experimental analysis. Journal of Political Economy, 97(5), 1232–1254.
  • Newton, E. L. (1990). The rocky road from actions to intentions (Doctoral dissertation). Stanford University.
