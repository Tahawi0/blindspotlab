Bias

Overconfidence

Being more sure of what we know and what we estimate than our actual accuracy justifies.

Where you'll meet it: Work and school Shopping and money Sports and out and about

In real life

On the road
In surveys, most drivers rate themselves as more skilled than most other drivers, which can't be true for most of us.
At work
“I'm 100% sure that number is right.” — Then someone double-checks, and it's wrong.
In investing
“I really understand the market.” — So he puts his entire 401(k) into a single stock.

How to spot it

Ask: how many times have I felt this sure and turned out wrong? And what could make me wrong this time?

How to respond without a fight

Instead of “I'm sure,” say “I'm about this sure.” And get a second opinion before big decisions.

Don't confuse it with: Dunning–Kruger Effect

Overconfidence is general and happens to everyone, experts included. Dunning–Kruger is a narrower, disputed claim: that people who know little overestimate themselves the most.

Strength of evidence

Strong

Test yourself

"I know this route, I don't need GPS." An hour later, you're all lost.

What's the trick here?

Confidence in the knowledge was bigger than the knowledge itself.

Sources

  • Moore, D. A., & Healy, P. J. (2008). The trouble with overconfidence. Psychological Review, 115(2), 502–517.
  • Svenson, O. (1981). Are we all less risky and more skillful than our fellow drivers? Acta Psychologica, 47(2), 143–148.
