Bias

Illusion of Control

We think we can influence things that are actually up to chance or outside our control.

Where you'll meet it: Home and family Shopping and money Sports and out and about

In real life

Money
“I’ll pick my own lottery numbers instead of a Quick Pick. That way my odds are better.” The drawing is random either way.
In sports
You sit in the same spot on the couch and wear the same jersey every game so your team will win.
At home
You press the elevator button five times in a row, as if that will make it come faster.

How to spot it

Ask: if I didn’t do this at all, would the outcome actually be different? Do I have evidence, or is it just a feeling?

How to respond without a fight

Separate what’s in your hands from what isn’t, and put your time and money into the first. If your rituals make you happy, keep them, just don’t bet money on them.

Don't confuse it with: Gambler's Fallacy

The gambler’s fallacy is thinking chance is “due” and will even out. The illusion of control is thinking you’re the one steering the chance.

Strength of evidence

Moderate — the effect exists, but its size varies a lot with the situation

Sources

  • Langer, E. J. (1975). The illusion of control. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 32(2), 311–328.
